# Not Responding To Name

## Summary

This concern describes a child who does not reliably turn, look up, or answer when their
name is called. It is an observation about responding, not a diagnosis, and it can have
several quite different explanations. What tends to be informative is whether it happens
occasionally or consistently, whether the child responds to other sounds, and how the
child responds in quiet settings compared with busy ones.

## Typical User Questions

- My child doesn't respond to his name.
- Why doesn't my daughter answer when I call her?
- My son ignores me when I call him.
- My child only responds to his name sometimes.
- Should my one year old respond to her name?
- My child hears the television but doesn't turn when called.
- Is not responding to name a sign of autism?
- My child looks away when I call him.
- Do I need to get my child's hearing checked?
- My toddler responds at home but not outside.
- My child responds to me but not to others.
- Everyone says he has selective hearing, is that a real thing?
- My child used to turn when called and now doesn't.
- Why does my child not look at me when I say his name?

## Alternative User Phrasings

- Doesn't answer when called.

- Ignores me completely.

## Possible Explanations

- Reduced hearing may be the reason, and even intermittent hearing loss from fluid or
  infection can make a called name hard to pick out.
- A child deeply absorbed in an activity may genuinely not register the sound, since
  attention filters what reaches awareness.
- Some children can find it harder to separate a voice from background noise, so a busy
  room may mask the call entirely.
- Responding to a name is partly a social skill, and for some children shared attention
  develops on a different timeline.
- A child who is still learning that the name refers specifically to them may respond
  inconsistently for a period.
- Tiredness, hunger or being unwell can temporarily reduce how readily any child responds
  to what is going on around them.
- Where responding is followed mostly by requests or instructions, some children may
  become slower to turn over time.
- Differences in how sound is processed can mean the name is heard but takes longer to
  register than the moment allows.

## Things to Observe

- Whether the child responds to other sounds, such as a favourite programme starting or a
  packet being opened.
- Whether the response differs when called from close by, from behind, or across a noisy
  room.
- Whether the child responds more readily to certain voices than to others.
- Whether the child connects in other ways, such as bringing objects over, pointing, or
  checking an adult's reaction.
- Whether responses have changed over recent months, in either direction.
- Whether the child turns when touched gently on the shoulder while being called.
- How the child responds when the call has nothing to do with an instruction.

## Everyday Support Ideas

- Families often find that calling from close by, at the child's eye level, gets a clearer
  response than calling across a room.
- Reducing competing noise before calling makes the sound easier to separate from the
  background.
- Using the name once and then waiting, rather than repeating it quickly, gives time for
  the sound to register.
- Pairing the name with something enjoyable rather than only with instructions keeps
  responding worthwhile.
- A light touch on the arm alongside the name can help when a child is absorbed in
  something.
- Noting the settings where responses come easily gives useful information for any later
  assessment.
- Celebrating a response when it comes, however delayed, tends to encourage more of them.

## ManaScience Perspective

- ManaScience treats responding to a name as a skill built from hearing, attention and
  social connection together, rather than as a single ability.
- The question of interest is what is making responding harder, not which label the
  behaviour might belong to.
- Hearing is looked at early, because it is straightforward to check and shapes everything
  built on top of it.
- Everyday interactions at home are where this skill is practised most, which puts
  families at the centre of the picture.

## Neuroplasticity Explanation

- The brain learns to pick out a familiar name from background sound through repeated
  experience, and that filtering ability continues to develop.
- Pathways for listening and for shared attention strengthen together, so support in one
  area often helps the other.
- Because these networks stay changeable, consistent everyday practice can make responding
  easier over time.
- Change here often shows up gradually and unevenly rather than as a sudden shift.

## Professional Boundary

- Manasi can provide educational information, but cannot diagnose medical conditions.
- Only a qualified healthcare professional can determine why a child is not responding to
  their name.
- A hearing assessment by an audiologist is usually the first step that can rule hearing in
  or out.
- Information here describes general possibilities and cannot describe what is happening
  for an individual child.

## When Professional Evaluation May Help

- When a hearing check has not yet been done, an audiology appointment may help provide
  more clarity.
- When a child rarely responds to their name in any setting, including quiet ones.
- When a child previously responded reliably and now does so much less.
- When limited responding appears alongside few gestures, little pointing, or reduced
  shared attention.
- When speech is also developing more slowly than expected for the child's age.
- When the pattern is causing difficulty at nursery, school or in daily routines.

## Common Misunderstandings

- Selective hearing is often described as deliberate, but attention and sound processing
  explain most of what families notice.
- Responding to a television or a snack packet does not confirm that hearing is fully
  typical across all pitches.
- A single missed response means very little on its own; the pattern across settings is
  what carries information.
- Repeating a name several times in quick succession usually makes responding harder
  rather than easier.

## What This Concern Is Not

- This concern is about responding when called, which is different from finding certain
  sounds distressing — see [distressed by loud sounds](../sensory/distressed_by_loud_sounds.md).
- It is not the same as a child who hears and understands but chooses not to comply with
  an instruction.
- It does not by itself describe autism, though shared attention is one of several areas
  professionals consider.

## Age-Specific Notes

- In the first year, responding to a familiar voice usually appears before responding
  reliably to a name.
- Between one and two years, most children turn towards their name in a quiet setting more
  often than not.
- From three years onwards, responding is expected across more settings, including busier
  ones with competing noise.
