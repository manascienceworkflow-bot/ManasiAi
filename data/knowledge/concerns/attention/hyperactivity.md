# Hyperactivity

## Summary

This concern describes a child who is in near-constant motion, finds sitting still hard,
and appears to have far more physical energy than others of a similar age. Activity levels
vary widely in childhood, and what reads as excessive depends partly on the setting and
what is expected there. A consistently high level of movement across the day is worth
understanding rather than simply enduring.

## Typical User Questions

- My child is hyperactive.
- My child cannot sit still for even five minutes.
- My son is running and climbing all day long.
- Why is my child so restless?
- Is my child too active for his age?
- My daughter never seems to get tired.
- My child cannot stay seated during meals.
- The teacher says my son is disruptive in class.
- Is being very active a sign of ADHD?
- My child fidgets constantly even when watching television.
- Why does my child bounce off the walls in the evening?
- My son finds it hard to sit through homework.
- Is high energy just part of being a toddler?
- My child is exhausting to keep up with all day.

## Possible Explanations

- Expectations for stillness sometimes exceed what is developmentally reasonable, since
  many young children genuinely cannot sit for long.
- Some children need a great deal of movement to feel organised and alert, and they seek
  it out because it helps them regulate.
- Sleep quality affects daytime activity strongly, and poor sleep can increase
  restlessness rather than reduce it.
- Attention and self-regulation develop gradually, so staying still may be harder for some
  children than for their peers.
- Anxiety can present as constant motion, particularly in settings a child finds
  demanding.
- A task that is too hard, too easy or unclear reliably produces restlessness, so the
  activity may be about the demand rather than the child.
- Limited opportunity for physical activity during the day can leave energy that surfaces
  later, often in the evening.
- Busy or noisy environments can raise arousal for some children, which may appear as
  increased movement.

## Things to Observe

- Whether the restlessness is present across settings, or mainly at home, at school, or in
  particular places.
- How much physical activity the day has included before the difficult periods.
- Whether the child can settle for activities they find genuinely interesting.
- How sleep is going, including how long it takes to fall asleep and how settled the night
  is.
- Whether movement increases before or after specific demands, such as homework or
  mealtimes.
- What the child does with the movement, such as seeking climbing, spinning, or crashing
  into cushions.
- Whether adults around the child describe it similarly, since a shared picture is more
  useful than one view.

## Everyday Support Ideas

- Families often find that movement built in before a seated task works better than
  movement offered as a reward afterwards.
- Shorter tasks with clear endpoints tend to be more achievable than long ones that
  require sustained stillness.
- Planned physical activity such as climbing, swinging or heavy play can reduce constant
  seeking by meeting the need deliberately.
- Predictable routines and clear expectations reduce the extra load of not knowing what
  comes next.
- Allowing some movement while listening, such as standing or fidgeting quietly, can
  support attention rather than compete with it.
- Protecting sleep routines matters, because tiredness and restlessness reinforce each
  other in both directions.
- Noticing when a child does settle gives useful information about what conditions help.

## ManaScience Perspective

- ManaScience treats activity level as information about how a child regulates, not as a
  behaviour to be suppressed.
- Attention, movement, sensory processing and sleep are looked at together, since each
  shapes the others in daily life.
- Areas of approach such as Neurofeedback and reflex integration work sit in this space as
  things families may explore, not as recommendations for an individual child.
- Matching any approach to a child requires a full profile and human review rather than a
  description alone.

## Neuroplasticity Explanation

- Attention and self-regulation are capacities that develop over years, supported by
  repeated everyday experience.
- The brain networks involved continue maturing well into adolescence, so a current
  difficulty describes the present rather than the ceiling.
- Regular physical activity, predictable routines and adequate sleep are the conditions in
  which these networks develop most readily.
- Change usually appears gradually, and often first in the settings where support is
  strongest.

## Professional Boundary

- Manasi can provide educational information, but cannot diagnose medical conditions.
- Only a qualified healthcare professional can determine what is behind a child's activity
  level.
- A paediatrician, psychologist or occupational therapist is usually the professional who
  assesses attention and activity.
- Manasi does not recommend, prescribe, or advise changing any therapy or medication for a
  child.

## When Professional Evaluation May Help

- When restlessness is affecting learning, friendships, or family life across more than
  one setting.
- When adults in different settings independently raise the same concern.
- When a family has been managing this for a while, an evaluation by an appropriate
  professional may help provide more clarity.
- When high activity occurs alongside difficulty with sleep, frequent upset, or sensory
  sensitivities.
- When a child seems distressed by their own difficulty in settling.
- When safety is a recurring worry because of climbing or running without apparent
  caution.

## Common Misunderstandings

- High activity is often read as an early answer about ADHD, which no single behaviour can
  provide on its own.
- Sugar is widely blamed for restlessness, although sleep, activity and setting usually
  explain far more of what families notice.
- Removing all movement rarely improves attention, and for many children it makes settling
  harder.
- A child who sits still for a favourite activity is not therefore able to sit still on
  demand, since interest and regulation are different things.

## What This Concern Is Not

- This concern is about activity level, not about frequent emotional outbursts — see
  [daily tantrums](../behaviour/daily_tantrums.md).
- It is different from movement driven mainly by sensory discomfort in noisy settings —
  see [distressed by loud sounds](../sensory/distressed_by_loud_sounds.md).
- It is not a description of intelligence or effort, both of which are separate matters
  entirely.

## Age-Specific Notes

- In the toddler years, near-constant movement is typical, and stillness for more than a
  few minutes is uncommon.
- In the preschool years, children usually begin managing short seated activities,
  particularly with adult support nearby.
- At school age, expectations for sitting rise sharply, which is often when a long-standing
  pattern first becomes a concern.

## Family Perspective Notes

- Parents and teachers may describe the same child differently, because the demands of home
  and classroom differ substantially.
- Grandparents may compare a child with memories of other children, which can make a
  typical activity level seem unusual or the reverse.
- Managing a highly active child across a whole day is genuinely tiring, and that fatigue
  is worth acknowledging in its own right.
